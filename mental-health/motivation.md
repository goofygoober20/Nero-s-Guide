# Motivation

## What is Motivation?

Motivation comes and goes. It's not something you're born with or without, and it's not a skill you learn - it's a feeling that changes, like emotions. It's common to know what you "should" do but not feel like doing it. That feeling often doesn't last.

Your brain notices your body's energy levels and decides how much effort to put in. You can influence this by taking action. There are two useful ways to think about it:

1. Learn how to encourage feelings of motivation and energy more often.
2. Learn how to do what's important even when you don't feel motivated.

It's important to distinguish between procrastination and losing joy (anhedonia). Procrastination is avoiding tasks because they feel stressful, boring, or hard - everyone does this. Anhedonia is not enjoying things you used to like, often linked to depression, where effort feels pointless.

The key insight: action first, motivation later. Waiting to "feel like it" often means waiting forever. Usually, starting an action is what creates the feeling of motivation or enjoyment. Even a small action can start a positive shift. If you've lost joy, you might need to push yourself for a while before the enjoyment starts to return.

#### Section Summary

- Motivation isn't fixed; it's a feeling that changes.
- Don't wait to feel motivated before you act.
- Learning to act even without motivation is key.
- Procrastination is common; anhedonia (losing joy) is often linked to depression.
- Take action first, and motivation often follows.

## Finding and Keeping Motivation

Motivation changes, so it helps to figure out what helps you feel motivated and what drains it. Pay attention to your own patterns.

**Move your body.** Feeling motivated often comes after you start moving. Find movement that's easy to start even when you don't feel like it. Make it enjoyable by adding friends or music so you look forward to it.

**Stay focused on your goal.** You lose steam if you forget why you started. Reconnect with your goals daily. A quick journal note each morning about your goal actions, and a quick reflection at night, keeps you focused and accountable.

**Keep it small.** Big tasks feel too hard. Focus on one small step. Don't try to change everything at once - setbacks are normal. Give yourself credit for effort and small successes. Feeling like you're making progress helps keep you going.

**Handle urges wisely.** Change often means saying no to urges that take you off track. You have more self-control when stress is low, so manage your sleep, food, and exercise. Moving your body strengthens willpower. Being tired uses up energy needed for self-control.

**Change how you see failure.** Fear of failing kills motivation, especially if failing leads to shame or harsh self-talk. Shame doesn't motivate - it makes you want to hide and give up. Being kind to yourself after messing up actually helps you try again and do better.

After a setback, notice what your self-talk sounds like. How does it make you feel? How would you talk to a friend in the same situation? Treating yourself with kindness, honesty, and encouragement is what actually gets you back on track.

#### Section Summary

- You can encourage feelings of motivation.
- Moving your body helps create motivation.
- Stay connected to your goals daily. Keep steps small and consistent.
- Manage stress to better handle urges and stick to plans.
- Shame hurts motivation; self-kindness helps. Change how you view failure.

## Doing Things When You Don't Feel Like It

Motivation isn't always there, so we need ways to act even when we lack it.

**Do the opposite of the urge.** Feelings come with urges - suggestions, not commands. You can choose to do the opposite of what the feeling pushes you to do, especially if the urge leads to unhelpful actions. Act based on your values, not just your feelings. Being aware gives you a moment between feeling an urge and acting on it, letting you choose differently.

**Build habits to get past the "ugh" feeling.** Make important actions automatic, like brushing your teeth. Doing something repeatedly makes it easier for your brain. When stressed, your brain takes the easiest path.

Tips for building habits:
- Make the new action easy to start, especially when you don't feel like it.
- Set up your space to support the habit.
- Make clear plans and use reminders.
- Reward yourself - feeling proud counts.
- Be clear why this habit matters and link it to your values.
- Make it part of who you are: "I'm someone who does this."

**Keep going long-term.** Staying with it requires energy. Balance work and rest to avoid burnout. Take real breaks - don't just fill them with more tasks or scrolling. Break big goals into smaller steps and celebrate reaching each one. That small boost fuels you for the next step.

**Use gratitude.** Focusing on gratitude creates internal rewards and restores energy. Try shifting from "I have to" to "I get to."

**Plan ahead for problems.** Think about what might trip you up and make a specific plan for how you'll handle it. Set things up to make the right choice easy and the wrong choice hard.

**Connect actions to your identity.** Ask yourself who you want to be. When you link actions to your desired identity, you act even when motivation is low because "this is just what I do now." Imagine your future self, benefiting from the good choices you make today.

#### Section Summary

- You can't always count on motivation. Learn to act based on values.
- Build habits through repetition. Plan ahead for difficulties.
- Balance effort with rest. Use small internal rewards.
- Link actions to your identity and connect with your future self.

## Starting Big Changes

Sometimes you know things need to change, but you're not sure what needs to change or how to start. That's normal.

Before fixing something, take time to understand the problem. Like a detective figuring out the case before making an arrest, therapy often helps with this deep dive. Your ability to reflect on your experiences helps you make sense of them and figure out what needs to change.

Journaling helps with this. Write about past situations - what happened, what you thought and felt, how you reacted, and what the results were. Just getting it down helps you see patterns. Noticing cycles in hindsight helps you catch them as they happen in the future, giving you a chance to choose differently.

Questions to explore problems:
- Describe what happened.
- What thoughts did you have then? How did they affect your feelings?
- What feelings did you notice? What set them off?
- What did you feel urged to do? How did you actually react? What happened next?

#### Section Summary

- It's normal not to know exactly what needs changing or how to start.
- Get to know the problem well before trying to solve it.
- Reflect on past experiences (journaling helps) to understand your patterns.
- Understanding past patterns helps you make different choices in the future.
- Be honest with yourself about how you might be keeping yourself stuck.
