## Getting Started {#getting-started}

### Choosing where to grow

Most vegetables and flowering plants need at least 6 hours of direct sunlight per day. Before digging or buying, spend a day observing which parts of your yard get full sun, partial shade, and full shade. This single step will save you a lot of frustration.

If you have limited space, containers and raised beds work extremely well. They give you full control over soil quality, drain better than in-ground beds, and warm up faster in spring, extending your growing season.

### What to grow first

Start with forgiving plants that produce quickly. Early success builds confidence and teaches you the basics before you tackle fussier crops.

<div class="card-grid-2">
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="leaf" :size="18" /> Easy vegetables</h4>
    <p>Lettuce, radishes, courgette, green beans, kale. Fast growing with minimal care.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="sun" :size="18" /> Easy herbs</h4>
    <p>Basil, chives, mint (in a pot), parsley. Useful, compact, and hard to kill.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="flower" :size="18" /> Easy flowers</h4>
    <p>Sunflowers, marigolds, nasturtiums, zinnias. Most grow from seed with little fuss.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="seedling" :size="18" /> Easy perennials</h4>
    <p>Lavender, echinacea, hostas, black-eyed Susans. Plant once and they return each year.</p>
  </div>
</div>

### Seeds vs. transplants

Starting from seed is cheaper and gives you access to more variety. Transplants (bought as small plants) are faster and better for crops like tomatoes and peppers that need a long growing season. For a beginner, a mix of both works well.

<div class="about-tip">
  <GuideIcon name="info" class="tip-icon" />
  <span>Check the seed packet or plant label for the days to maturity. Compare that number against your last frost date to make sure you have enough growing season before cold weather arrives.</span>
</div>

## Soil and Beds {#soil}

### Understanding your soil

Soil type affects drainage, nutrient retention, and how easy it is to work. The three main types are:

<div class="card-grid-3">
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="layer" :size="18" /> Sandy soil</h4>
    <p>Drains fast, warms up quickly. Nutrients wash out easily. Improve it with compost.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="layer" :size="18" /> Clay soil</h4>
    <p>Holds water and nutrients well but compacts and drains poorly. Break it up with compost and grit.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="layer" :size="18" /> Loamy soil</h4>
    <p>The ideal mix of sand, silt, and clay. Well draining and nutrient rich. Most gardens need some work to get here.</p>
  </div>
</div>

A simple test: grab a handful of damp soil and squeeze. Sandy soil falls apart. Clay soil holds a solid shape. Loam holds together but crumbles when poked.

### Improving your soil

The answer to almost every soil problem is the same: add organic matter. Compost, aged manure, and leaf mould all improve structure, drainage, and nutrient content regardless of your starting soil type. Work it into the top 15 to 20cm before planting.

### Raised beds

Raised beds are the best starting point for most beginners. You fill them with exactly the mix you want, they drain well, and they warm up faster in spring. A simple mix for a raised bed is one third compost, one third topsoil, and one third a material like perlite or coarse sand for drainage.

Keep beds narrow enough to reach the centre from either side without stepping in. 1.2 metres wide is a good rule of thumb.

## Watering {#watering}

### How much and how often

The most common beginner mistake is watering too often but not deeply enough. Shallow watering keeps moisture at the surface, which is where it evaporates fastest and where it encourages weak, shallow root systems.

Instead, water deeply but less often. For most vegetable gardens, two or three deep waterings per week is better than a light sprinkle every day. Stick your finger 5cm into the soil. If it is dry at that depth, water. If it is still moist, wait.

### Best time to water

Water in the morning where possible. This gives leaves time to dry during the day, which reduces the risk of fungal disease. Evening watering leaves moisture on foliage overnight. Mid-day watering wastes water to evaporation.

### Rainwater

Rainwater is genuinely better for your garden than tap water. It is slightly acidic, which most plants prefer, and it contains dissolved nitrogen and other trace minerals that irrigation water lacks. If you notice a growth spurt after rainfall, this is why. A simple water butt attached to a downpipe is one of the best low-cost additions you can make to any garden.

### Watering methods

<div class="steps-grid">
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">1</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Drip irrigation</strong>
      <p>Most efficient. Water goes directly to roots with minimal evaporation. Worth setting up for beds you rely on.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">2</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Soaker hose</strong>
      <p>Runs along the base of plants. Cheaper than drip irrigation and does a similar job.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">3</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Watering can</strong>
      <p>Gives you the most control. Use a rose attachment to avoid disturbing seedlings.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">4</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Overhead sprinkler</strong>
      <p>Convenient but least efficient. Fine for lawns, less ideal for vegetables and flowers.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

## Composting {#composting}

Composting is the single highest-return habit you can build as a gardener. It turns kitchen scraps and garden waste into rich, free fertiliser while diverting material from landfill.

### What to compost

<div class="card-grid-2">
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="check" :size="18" /> Greens (nitrogen rich)</h4>
    <p>Fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, fresh grass clippings, plant trimmings, eggshells.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="check" :size="18" /> Browns (carbon rich)</h4>
    <p>Cardboard, paper, dry leaves, straw, wood chips, paper bags, newspaper.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="about-tip">
  <GuideIcon name="alert" class="tip-icon" />
  <span>Do not compost meat, fish, dairy, cooked food, diseased plants, or pet waste. These attract pests and can introduce pathogens to your garden.</span>
</div>

### The right balance

A good compost pile needs roughly equal amounts of greens and browns by volume. Too many greens and the pile becomes wet and smelly. Too many browns and it breaks down very slowly. If your pile smells, add more browns and turn it. If it is not heating up, add more greens and moisture.

### Hot vs. cold composting

<div class="card-grid-2">
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="flame" :size="18" /> Hot composting</h4>
    <p>Actively managed. You turn the pile regularly to introduce oxygen, which speeds decomposition. Ready in 1 to 3 months. Kills weed seeds and pathogens.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="clock" :size="18" /> Cold composting</h4>
    <p>Passive. You add material and leave it. Takes 6 to 12 months but requires almost no effort. Best for most home gardeners.</p>
  </div>
</div>

### How to build your pile

<div class="steps-grid">
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">1</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Start with a base</strong>
      <p>Lay down a 10cm layer of coarse browns like twigs or wood chips. This helps drainage and airflow from the bottom.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">2</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Layer greens and browns</strong>
      <p>Alternate layers of green and brown material. Keep each layer 5 to 10cm thick.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">3</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Keep it moist</strong>
      <p>The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge. Too dry and it will not break down. Too wet and it will smell.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">4</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Turn it</strong>
      <p>For hot composting, turn every 1 to 2 weeks. For cold, turn occasionally when you remember.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">5</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Use it when ready</strong>
      <p>Finished compost is dark, crumbly, and smells earthy. You should not be able to identify the original materials.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

## Pests and Problems {#pests}

Most garden problems are preventable with good habits rather than reactive treatments.

### Prevention first

<div class="steps-grid">
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">1</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Healthy soil = healthy plants</strong>
      <p>Plants grown in rich, well-draining soil are more resistant to pests and disease than stressed plants.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">2</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Good airflow</strong>
      <p>Do not overcrowd plants. Air circulation between plants reduces fungal disease dramatically.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">3</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Rotate crops</strong>
      <p>Do not grow the same family of plants in the same spot two years in a row. It breaks pest and disease cycles in the soil.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">4</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Encourage beneficial insects</strong>
      <p>Ladybirds, lacewings, and hoverflies eat aphids and other pests. Plant flowers alongside vegetables to attract them.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

### Common problems and fixes

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow leaves | Overwatering, nutrient deficiency | Check soil drainage, add compost |
| Leggy seedlings | Not enough light | Move closer to a window or add a grow light |
| Wilting despite watering | Root rot or compacted soil | Improve drainage, reduce watering |
| Holes in leaves | Caterpillars or slugs | Hand pick at night, use copper tape or mesh |
| White powder on leaves | Powdery mildew | Improve airflow, remove affected leaves |
| Aphid clusters | Common on new growth | Blast off with water, introduce ladybirds |
| Tomato leaves turning yellow | Many causes including blight | Check for brown patches, remove affected leaves, avoid wetting foliage |

### Companion planting

Some plants actively protect their neighbours. Planting them together reduces pest pressure naturally without any sprays.

<div class="card-grid-2">
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="leaf" :size="18" /> Marigolds</h4>
    <p>Planted near tomatoes and brassicas, they deter aphids, whitefly, and nematodes. One of the most useful companion plants.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="leaf" :size="18" /> Basil</h4>
    <p>Grown next to tomatoes, basil is said to repel aphids and improve flavour. Also deters thrips.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="leaf" :size="18" /> Nasturtiums</h4>
    <p>Act as a trap crop, attracting aphids away from vegetables. Also edible and easy to grow from seed.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="shared-card">
    <h4><GuideIcon name="leaf" :size="18" /> Chives</h4>
    <p>Planted near carrots and roses, they deter aphids and carrot fly. Attractive flowers too.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## Seasonal Tasks {#seasonal}

### Spring

Spring is the most active season. Soil temperature drives everything. Most seeds need soil above 10 degrees to germinate reliably.

<div class="steps-grid">
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">1</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Prepare beds</strong>
      <p>Dig in compost and remove weeds before planting. Do not work soil when it is waterlogged or it will compact.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">2</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Start seeds indoors</strong>
      <p>Tomatoes, peppers, aubergines, and courgettes need an early start indoors 6 to 8 weeks before your last frost date.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">3</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Direct sow hardy crops</strong>
      <p>Once soil is workable, sow lettuce, radishes, spinach, and peas directly where they will grow.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">4</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Harden off transplants</strong>
      <p>Before moving indoor seedlings outside permanently, put them outside for a few hours a day for a week to acclimatise.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

### Summer

Summer is about maintenance and harvest. The main jobs are watering, feeding, and staying on top of weeds before they set seed.

Mulching in early summer is one of the highest-value tasks you can do. A 5 to 7cm layer of compost, straw, or wood chips around plants retains moisture, suppresses weeds, and slowly feeds the soil as it breaks down.

Harvest regularly. For most crops, the more you pick, the more the plant produces. Leaving courgettes to become marrows, for example, signals the plant to stop producing.

### Autumn

Autumn is about tidying up, planting for next year, and putting the garden to bed well.

<div class="steps-grid">
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">1</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Clear spent crops</strong>
      <p>Remove finished vegetable plants. Compost healthy material, bin or burn anything with disease.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">2</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Plant garlic and spring bulbs</strong>
      <p>Garlic goes in autumn for a summer harvest. Tulips, daffodils, and alliums all go in now too.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">3</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Collect leaves</strong>
      <p>Fallen leaves make excellent leaf mould after a year or two in a pile. Do not throw them away.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="step-card">
    <span class="step-number">4</span>
    <div>
      <strong>Add compost to beds</strong>
      <p>Spread a thick layer of compost over empty beds and leave it. Worms will work it in over winter.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

### Winter

For most gardens, winter is the quietest time. Use it to plan next year, order seeds, maintain tools, and build or repair beds and structures.

Avoid walking on beds when the soil is wet or frozen. Compaction when soil is waterlogged damages structure that takes months to recover.
