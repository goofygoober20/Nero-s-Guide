# Low Mood & Self-Care

## Getting to Know Low Mood

Low mood happens to everyone. We all have down days, and how often or how intensely we feel them varies. Many people hide their low mood because of pressure from others or comparing themselves to seemingly happy people. But the truth is, your mood can change - it's not fixed.

Like your body temperature, mood is affected by things inside you (like not getting enough sleep or water) and things around you (like stress or your environment). It's not just a "brain problem" or who you are. Science shows we have more control over our feelings than we might think, which means we can take steps to feel better.

We can't get rid of low mood completely - life is hard sometimes - but we can learn skills and tools to handle it better. These are useful for everyone, not just those with diagnosed depression.

### How Feelings Start

Your body and mind are linked. Feelings aren't just in your head - your physical state (tired, thirsty), surroundings, actions, thoughts, and memories all send signals to your brain. Your brain gathers these clues, compares them to past experiences, and makes its best guess about what's happening, which you feel as a mood or emotion.

This goes both ways: thoughts affect feelings, and feelings affect thoughts. When you feel low, you're more likely to have negative thoughts, making "just think positive" advice hard to follow. Your actions (or lack of action) also create cycles - feeling low, doing less, feeling lower.

We usually experience thoughts, feelings, body sensations, and actions all mixed together. Looking at them separately helps figure out what's affecting your mood and where you can make changes.

### Where to Start

Start by noticing more. Look back at your day and notice the different parts of an experience - thoughts, body feelings, actions, surroundings. This helps you get better at noticing these things as they happen.

Ask yourself questions to see the connections:
- What was I thinking when I felt that way?
- How did my body feel?
- What did I do just before?
- Is this feeling an emotion, or do I just need something (like food or rest)?

Think about what adds to low mood by using journal prompts:
- What happened right before?
- What thoughts came up?
- What did you focus on?
- What feelings were there? How did your body feel?
- What did you feel like doing? What did you actually do?
- How did your actions affect your feelings and thoughts?

#### Section Summary

- Feeling low sometimes is normal; no one is happy constantly.
- Low mood often signals unmet needs, not just a brain issue.
- Your thoughts, feelings, body, and actions are linked and create cycles.
- You can influence feelings indirectly by changing things you control (thoughts, body care, actions).
- Tools like breaking down experiences help you see what affects your mood.

## Common Mood Traps

### The Problem with Quick Fixes

When feeling low, we often reach for things that give fast relief - numbing with food or alcohol, distracting with TV or social media. They work for a bit, but the bad feelings often return stronger later, creating a negative cycle.

Quick fixes cost us more in the long run, in terms of time, health, and making progress. Real, lasting helpful strategies usually aren't quick.

Think about how you cope:
- What do you usually do when you feel low?
- Does it help instantly?
- What happens later on? What does it cost you (time, health)?

### Thought Patterns That Make Mood Worse

How you feel affects the kind of thoughts you have. Low mood makes common "thinking traps" more likely. These happen to everyone but are more common when mood is down.

Common thinking traps:
- **Guessing what others think (negatively):** Assuming you know others' negative thoughts about you without proof. It makes you seek reassurance.
- **Making big judgments from one event:** Seeing one bad thing as a sign everything will go wrong.
- **Thinking everyone sees things your way:** Assuming others share your views and judging them when they don't.
- **Believing feelings are facts:** Thinking something must be true just because you feel it strongly.
- **Focusing only on the negatives:** Noticing only the bad stuff, ignoring anything positive.
- **Strict rules for yourself ("musts" and "shoulds"):** Having rigid expectations that set you up to feel like a failure.
- **Seeing things as black or white:** Thinking in extremes with no grey areas.

### What to Do About Thinking Traps

The goal is to spot these traps, see thoughts as ideas rather than facts, and choose how you react. Awareness is the first step, though it's harder when you're upset because feelings and thoughts are jumbled.

Practical approaches:
- **Look back first:** Journal about past moments and identify thinking traps after things have calmed down.
- **Use distancing language:** Say "I'm having the thought that..." instead of "I am...". Or "I'm noticing the feeling of..." This creates space.
- **Ask a trusted friend:** Gently ask someone supportive if they notice patterns in your thinking.
- **Practice mindfulness:** Learn to observe thoughts without getting caught up in them.

Key ideas to remember:
- Giving a thinking trap a name helps reduce its power.
- This takes ongoing practice.
- Aim for more balanced and kind ways of seeing things, not just the one "right" thought.
- It's okay not to know or decide immediately.
- Spotting these traps can stop small upsets from turning into major mood dips.

#### Section Summary

- Thinking traps are normal but make low moods worse if you believe them without question.
- We tend to look for proof for our beliefs, even negative ones.
- Low moods often make us focus on possible threats or losses.
- Strategy 1: Remember feelings aren't proof that your thoughts are true.
- Strategy 2: Be curious about your thoughts.
- Strategy 3: Step back by noticing and naming thinking traps.

## Helpful Tools and Ideas

### Getting Some Distance (Thinking About Your Thinking)

Overwhelming thoughts are like wearing a mask - they colour how you see everything. Getting distance (like holding the mask away from your face) lessens their control. Thinking about your thinking means noticing your thoughts. Stepping back lets you see them for what they are: opinions, stories, guesses - not facts.

Thoughts aren't facts. They're just your brain's interpretations, based on limited information and mental shortcuts. Mindfulness helps you practice watching thoughts come and go without getting stuck, helping you choose where to focus.

### Mindfulness: Choosing Your Focus

You can't stop thoughts from popping up, but you can choose where you put your attention - your mental "spotlight." Decide which thoughts get your main attention, instead of letting your focus drift or get stuck on negativity.

Difficult things grab our attention, but we also need to focus on how we want to feel or what we want to move towards. Ask yourself: "What do I want instead?"

Meditation is like practice for your mind, building the muscle for directing attention. Consistency matters more than having amazing experiences every time.

### How to Stop Getting Stuck on Negative Thoughts (Rumination)

Rumination is going over and over negative thoughts or memories. It makes bad feelings stronger and lasts longer, keeping depression going. To break the cycle:

- Notice you're stuck, say "Stop!" in your head or out loud, then move your body - stand up, walk around, change rooms.
- Ask a helpful question: "What would the best version of me do right now?" This gives you a positive direction, even if you can only take a small step.
- Connect with people. Talking to supportive friends or a therapist can interrupt the negative thought cycle and offer new perspectives.

### Mindfulness & Gratitude Practice

A mindful state means paying attention to right now - thoughts, feelings, body sensations - without judging. It helps you respond thoughtfully instead of automatically. Formal meditation is like a workout for your mind.

Gratitude practice is a simple way to focus your attention positively. Every day, write down three things you're thankful for, big or small. Think about how they make you feel. This trains your attention over time.

#### Section Summary

- You control your attention, not which thoughts pop up.
- Trying not to think of something usually makes you think of it more.
- Allow thoughts, but choose where you focus - this affects feelings.
- Mindfulness and gratitude help train your attention.
- Focus on where you want to go, not just problems.
- Thoughts are suggestions, not facts. Believing them gives them power.

## Making Bad Days Better

Low mood makes decisions hard. It pushes you towards unhelpful actions and makes helpful ones seem too difficult. Trying to be perfect makes it worse.

Aim for "good enough" choices. Focus on small steps in the right direction, not the perfect solution. Taking action is what matters. Make choices based on what's truly important to you, not just how you feel right now. Ask yourself: What matters? Am I acting on it? What's one small step today?

Pick one tiny, doable change you can make every day. Consistency builds habits, and slow change lasts longer.

Be kind to yourself. Low mood often brings more self-criticism, which doesn't help. Find your kind voice - how would you talk to a loved one going through this? Try talking to yourself that way. Self-kindness is the supportive voice you need to get back up, not being lazy. It's like having a good coach.

Instead of just focusing on feelings you don't want, think about the positive feelings you'd like more of. What actions and thoughts go with those feelings?

The "miracle question" can help: Imagine your main problem vanished overnight. What's the very first sign? What would you do, say, or focus on differently? This creates a positive vision and might show small changes you can make now.

#### Section Summary

- Aim for "good enough" decisions to avoid getting stuck. Action matters.
- Make small, consistent changes.
- Practice self-kindness; it helps more than being hard on yourself.
- Once you understand the problem, focus on where you want to go.

## Looking After Yourself: The Basics

Simple self-care things like sleep, exercise, food, routine, and human connection are like your body's defense team for mental health. We often neglect them when feeling down, but they're vital. Aim for good enough, not perfection.

**Exercise** has strong mood-lifting effects. It releases chemicals that increase your ability to enjoy things. Focus on enjoyment and how moving feels, not just how it looks. Being active in nature is extra helpful. Start small, find movement you like so you'll stick with it.

**Sleep** is crucial. Not enough sleep makes anyone feel worse mentally and physically. Poor mental health affects sleep, and poor sleep affects mental health - they go together. Tips: try for a regular sleep time, get sunlight in the morning, keep lights dim at night, deal with worries during the day, avoid caffeine late, and focus on relaxing (you can't force sleep).

**Nutrition** matters because your brain and body are connected. Eating better can help with depression symptoms. Focus on making one small, healthy food choice at a time rather than overhauling everything at once.

**Routine** helps us feel safe, but we also need some change. Small changes in routine add up. Find a balance of structure and flexibility that works for you.

**Connecting with people** is a powerful protector of mental health. Depression makes you want to hide, but connecting with others lifts mood and gives perspective. Fight the urge to isolate. Connecting doesn't always mean deep talks - just being around others helps.

#### Section Summary

- The basics (exercise, sleep, food, routine, connection) are key defenses for your well-being.
- Find enjoyable ways to move your body.
- Prioritize good sleep habits.
- Nourish your brain with whole foods.
- Keep some routine but allow for changes.
- Connect with others; fight the urge to withdraw.
