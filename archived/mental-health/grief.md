# Grief

## What is Grief?

Grief isn't just about death. You can feel grief after any significant ending or loss - losing a job, a relationship ending, or major life changes. It's the natural human way of responding when we lose someone or something important that we loved or relied on. It's not a weakness or something to fix quickly.

Grief can include deep longing, physical feelings like aches or exhaustion, and activation of your body's stress response. The pain can feel both emotional and physical.

Trying to block out the pain might feel safer at first, but unresolved grief can lead to problems later - depression, unhealthy coping habits, or feeling numb. Sometimes small things can trigger big waves of pushed-away grief later.

Grief can feel huge and scary, but understanding it's a process helps. You can learn to dip into the feeling, experience it, breathe, and then step back to rest. Over time, you build strength to handle the waves and still engage with life.

#### Section Summary

- Grief can happen after any major loss, not just death.
- It's a normal and necessary human response involving mind and body.
- Trying to avoid grief completely can cause problems down the road.
- Working through grief involves allowing yourself to feel the pain in doses, learning you can survive it, and gradually re-engaging with life.

## Common Experiences in Grief

Ideas like denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance describe common feelings in grief, but they don't happen in a set order or for a set time. They're not rules for how grief "should" look.

Common feelings and experiences:
- **Denial or shock:** Feeling numb or finding it hard to believe at first. This helps you survive the initial overwhelming pain. It fades over time, letting other feelings surface.
- **Anger:** Often covers up deep pain or fear. Letting yourself feel and express anger safely, such as through exercise, can help release tension.
- **Bargaining:** Thinking "What if..." or "If only...". Trying to mentally undo the loss. Can sometimes lead to blaming yourself.
- **Depression (sadness/emptiness):** Feeling deep sadness or emptiness after a loss. This is a normal reaction, not necessarily a mental illness. It needs comfort and support, not being told to "snap out of it."
- **Acceptance:** Not meaning you like or approve of the loss, but starting to accept the new reality and finding ways to live within it. This isn't a final step - grief comes in waves, and you might revisit other feelings.

#### Section Summary

- Common grief experiences happen, but not in neat stages.
- Denial protects early on. Anger needs safe release. Deep sadness is normal. Acceptance means adjusting to the new reality.
- Grief feelings come and go in waves.

## Working Through Grief

There are four tasks that describe what grief involves: accepting the loss is real, feeling and working through the pain, adjusting to life without the person or thing lost, and finding a way to stay connected while also moving forward.

Grief often involves moving back and forth between focusing on the loss (feeling the pain, remembering, crying) and focusing on restoration (dealing with life changes, distracting yourself, taking breaks). You need both.

Allowing yourself time and space to feel the emotions that come up - through talking, writing, or crying - helps process the grief. Balance this with healthy ways to calm down afterwards. Constantly staying busy to avoid the pain means you never truly rest or process the loss.

It's okay to feel despair, anger, and confusion. It's also okay to feel moments of joy or laugh at something. Feeling okay sometimes doesn't mean you've forgotten.

When everything feels overwhelming, focus on tiny, achievable steps each day. Don't put pressure on yourself about how long grief should take or how it should look. Be kind and patient with yourself.

Find safe ways to let feelings out: talk to someone trusted, write, draw, listen to music, or move your body. The pain of loss doesn't necessarily disappear, but you can build a meaningful life around that pain. Pain and joy can exist together.

#### Section Summary

- Working through grief involves accepting, feeling the pain, adjusting, and finding new ways to stay connected while living.
- Move between focusing on the loss and focusing on rebuilding life; both are needed.
- Allow all feelings, take small steps, drop expectations, express yourself safely, connect with others.
- Build a new life around the pain of loss. Seek professional help if needed.

## Finding Strength in Grief

There are areas to focus on that help rebuild your life during grief. They need ongoing attention:

1. **Relationship with the person who died.** Find new ways to feel connected and keep their memory alive.
2. **Relationship with yourself.** Pay attention to your own needs and well-being. Seek support when needed.
3. **Expressing grief.** Let yourself feel what comes up and find ways to express it.
4. **Time.** Be patient. There's no set timeline. Focus on one day at a time.
5. **Mind and body connection.** Take care of your physical health and stay connected with supportive people.
6. **Setting limits.** It's okay to set boundaries around others' advice or expectations about your grief.
7. **Structure and routine.** Keep some daily structure for stability, but allow flexibility to grieve when you need to.
8. **Paying attention inside.** Notice body sensations connected to your feelings, especially when words are hard to find.

#### Section Summary

- Build strength by keeping connection with the deceased, caring for yourself, expressing grief, allowing time, looking after mind and body, setting boundaries, maintaining some structure, and noticing internal feelings.
