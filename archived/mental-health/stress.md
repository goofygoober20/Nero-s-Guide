# Stress & Performance

## Stress vs. Anxiety: What's the Difference?

People use "stress" and "anxiety" for many different feelings. Both involve the body getting ready for action, but they feel different.

Anxiety often feels more like fear - worrying about something bad happening. It might feel like fight, flight, or freeze. Stress is more a general feeling of pressure when demands feel high. It can feel positive (excitement before a race) or negative (overwhelmed by deadlines).

Your brain releases energy and increases alertness to help you meet demands. When your body gears up like this, it expects to get rest or fuel back afterwards. If stress is constant and you don't recharge, your body's resources get low.

#### Section Summary

- Anxiety is more fear-based; stress is more pressure-based.
- Both involve the body preparing for action.
- Stress itself isn't bad; it helps us perform. Problems arise when stress is constant without recovery.

## More Than Just Reducing Stress

We can't always remove stressors - life throws unavoidable challenges. We also sometimes choose stress, like enjoying exciting experiences or taking on meaningful challenges. A life with no stress would be boring.

Short bursts of stress can boost your immune system, sharpen your focus, and give you energy to perform well. How you think about stress matters. Seeing the physical signs of stress (like a faster heartbeat) as your body helping you perform, rather than as a sign you're failing, changes how you experience it.

The goal is to manage and recover. Learn to use the helpful parts of stress when needed, calm down when not needed, and most importantly, focus on recovering after stressful periods.

#### Section Summary

- You can't always eliminate stress, and some stress is good for performance.
- Focus on managing your stress response and making time to recover afterwards.
- Think of stress signs as your body helping you meet challenges.

## When Stress Becomes Harmful

Chronic stress happens when your body's stress response stays activated for too long without enough breaks to recover. It wears your system down, making it harder to control impulses, remember things, and make good decisions.

**Burnout** is a state of exhaustion from prolonged stress. Signs include:
- Emotional exhaustion
- Detachment or cynicism
- Feeling ineffective or like you're not accomplishing anything

Burnout often involves lack of control, feeling unrewarded, lack of community, unfairness, or doing work that clashes with your values.

Other signs of chronic stress or burnout: ongoing sleep problems, appetite changes, irritability, trouble concentrating, inability to relax, frequent headaches, relying more on substances like smoking or drinking.

Check in with yourself: How often do you feel drained? Do you dread the day? Do you have energy for enjoyable things? Are you getting sick often? Do your efforts feel worthwhile?

#### Section Summary

- Stress becomes harmful when it's long-lasting without enough recovery time.
- Burnout is serious exhaustion from prolonged stress. Look for signs like feeling drained, detached, and ineffective.
- Address burnout by finding ways to balance demands with rest and recharging.

## Using Stress in Helpful Ways & Managing Overwhelm

**Use your breath.** Breathing techniques that calm anxiety also work for stress. To calm down, make your out-breath longer. To energize, take deeper, more vigorous in-breaths.

**Connect with others.** Your stress response includes an urge to connect with and protect others. Focusing on caring for others during stressful times builds resilience. Good social connections buffer stress; isolation makes it worse.

**Focus on goals bigger than yourself.** Having goals related to contributing to others boosts positive feelings. Briefly thinking about how your efforts help others can reduce your body's stress reaction.

**Use meditation.** It changes the brain, reduces stress, and improves emotion regulation. Even short sessions help. Practice mindfulness during everyday tasks - walking, showering, brushing your teeth.

**Seek awe.** Encountering vastness - nature, art, ideas - helps detach from small stresses and gain perspective.

### Managing Overwhelm

When you feel stuck or tense, use grounding to reconnect to the present. Notice things you see, hear, and touch. Describe them mentally. Combine with self-soothing.

Overwhelm often comes with thoughts like "I can't cope." Notice this storyline without automatically believing it. Reframe from "I can't cope" to "How can I prepare or bring my best?"

Feeling overwhelmed is a signal that your life setup isn't working or needs aren't being met - it's not proof of inadequacy.

Set aside protected time just to be present without tasks. Step out of the chaos to replenish.

Learn to shift your state. If overwhelmed and tense, use calming breath. If feeling flat but need to act, use energizing breath and movement. Awareness of what shifts your state is key.

#### Section Summary

- Overwhelm doesn't mean inadequacy. Resist judging yourself; get curious.
- Check limiting beliefs and reframe toward action or preparation.
- Learn to both calm and energize yourself as needed (breathwork, movement).
- Practice grounding and non-doing time to replenish.

## Handling High-Pressure Moments

The challenge is how to cope with stress during important, unavoidable moments - tests, interviews, performances - where you need to do well. Relaxation isn't the goal here. Instead, learn to use the energy and focus from stress positively.

**Mindset matters.** Believing the stress response (fast heart, alertness) is helping you perform better actually improves performance and confidence. Think "use this energy" rather than "try despite the stress."

**Watch your language.** Avoid focusing on what not to do ("Don't mess up"). Create effective mantras - short, specific, believable, focused on the process. Prepare them beforehand.

**Reframe the feeling.** Call stress "determination" or anxiety "excitement." Labeling the feeling differently shifts your emotional response without denying the physical sensations.

**Use your vision.** Stress causes tunnel vision. Intentionally widening your gaze - looking around softly without moving your head - can calm your mind while keeping you alert.

**Focus on process, not outcome.** Worrying about failing uses mental energy needed for performing. Focus intensely on the steps of what you need to do, leaving no mental space for worrying about the result.

#### Section Summary

- Manage high-pressure stress by shifting mindset (stress as asset).
- Use effective self-talk (process-focused, factual).
- Reframe threat as challenge.
- Focus on process over outcome, and welcome anxiety signs as readiness.
