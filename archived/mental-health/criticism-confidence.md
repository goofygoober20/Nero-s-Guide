# Criticism, Confidence & Self-Worth

## Handling Criticism, Difficult Friendships & Arguments

Caring what others think is normal. We're wired to care about belonging and avoiding rejection. Telling yourself "I don't care" rarely works long-term.

People-pleasing - putting others' needs first so much it harms your own well-being - can lead to difficulty saying no, setting boundaries, and feeling resentful. The spotlight effect means people notice and judge us much less than we think. Most people are focused on themselves.

When handling criticism, your goals should be:
- Take useful feedback on board for growth without letting it crush your self-worth.
- Learn from mistakes without feeling destroyed.
- Ignore criticism that's about the other person's rules or values, not yours.
- Decide whose opinions genuinely matter to you and why.

Highly critical people are often hard on themselves too. Their criticism might reflect their own strict rules, not an objective truth about you.

**Guilt vs. shame:** Guilt is about doing something wrong and can lead to fixing it. Shame is about being wrong or bad and makes you want to hide. Building resilience to shame involves knowing your triggers, checking facts, managing replays, practicing self-kindness, and talking safely.

### Difficult Friendships

True friends offer honest feedback aligned with your values, even if it's difficult. Criticism laced with contempt or aimed at diminishing you is different. Ask yourself: is this helpful, or is it about them?

Ending friendships is hard but sometimes necessary. Consider patterns: Do they compete? Talk negatively? Exclude you? How do you feel with them? Don't stay in harmful friendships out of fear of loneliness. Bad friendships harm well-being.

Reflect honestly on your own role: Are you contributing positively? Listening? Supporting? Take responsibility for your side.

### Handling Passive Aggression

Passive aggression shows up as indirect hostility - sarcasm, backhanded compliments, sulking, exclusion, gossip, subtle digs. It often stems from fear, insecurity, or an inability to be direct.

How to respond: Stay calm and focus on your values. Don't get sucked into power games. Direct confrontation often leads to denial or arguments. Sometimes subtly noting something is off ("You seem unlike yourself") shows awareness without fueling drama. Get distance if needed.

### Resolving Conflict

Aim for resolution and maintaining the relationship, not winning. Truly try to understand the other person's perspective before planning your response. Feeling heard de-escalates conflict.

Avoid personal attacks, contempt, and name-calling. Be flexible but have boundaries. Slow down - composure is your superpower. Acknowledge their feelings without needing to agree with their viewpoint. Watch for your own biases: confirmation bias, emotional reasoning, and overgeneralizing.

#### Section Summary

- Handle criticism by filtering feedback through your values and nurturing self-worth.
- Navigate difficult friendships by distinguishing constructive feedback from harm, setting boundaries, and reflecting on your role.
- Approach arguments aiming for resolution, not victory. Listen first, avoid personal attacks, and watch for your own biases.

## Building Confidence & Overcoming Imposter Feelings

Confidence isn't fixed - it changes depending on the situation. Entering new territory means feeling unsure at first, and facing that uncertainty builds courage for the next time.

Confidence grows outside your comfort zone. You build it by doing things before you feel confident. Courage comes first. Building confidence involves learning to tolerate fear, not getting rid of it. Think in terms of three zones: comfort, stretch (growth), and panic. Aim for the stretch zone.

### Feeling Like an Imposter

Feeling like an imposter is normal, especially when stepping up or learning. High achievers often feel it most. It's a problem only if you let it stop you.

Ways to deal with imposter thoughts:
- **Spot the bias.** Notice thoughts focusing on incompetence or perceived judgment. Recognize when you're personalizing - blaming your flaws for all mistakes.
- **Be honest.** Acknowledge your beginner status or lack of preparation openly. Be willing to learn and ask questions. Be a "self-declared work in progress."
- **Focus on self-respect over self-esteem.** Hold yourself as valuable while learning, accept imperfection, and contribute where you can.
- **Make fair comparisons.** Compare to those slightly ahead for inspiration, not world experts. Don't compare your beginning to their middle.
- **Check your expectations.** Are they realistic for your current stage? Perfectionism fuels imposter feelings.

### The Recipe for Confidence Building

1. **Put in the reps.** Practice the necessary skills consistently. This builds brain pathways.
2. **Stress test.** Test your skills under increasing but manageable pressure that mimics the real challenge.
3. **Examine performance.** Reflect on what worked and what needs improvement. Treat feedback as valuable data.
4. **Turn insights into action.** Actively practice areas needing work. Repeat the cycle.

Create a hierarchy of feared situations and start practicing with manageable ones. Visualization can also help - imagine you woke up confident. What actions would you take differently? Focus on doing those actions now.

#### Section Summary

- Build confidence by taking courageous action in your stretch zone.
- Focus on self-respect, process, and learning from failure.
- Feeling like an imposter is normal. Counter it with honesty, fair comparisons, realistic expectations, and focusing on progress.

## Mistakes Don't Define You (Dealing with Regret & Inner Critic)

How we handle mistakes greatly affects our self-doubt. Others' reactions to your mistakes say more about their relationship with failure than about your worth. Constantly playing it safe brings temporary relief but keeps you stuck.

### Bouncing Back from Failure

1. **Notice** how failure or regret feels - the urges, the blocking behaviours.
2. **Stop rumination.** Call a stop to replaying painful thoughts. Shift to productive thinking: How can I make this better? What can I learn?
3. **Step back and allow feelings.** Label emotions. See thoughts as stories. Be curious. Let feelings wash over while being kind to yourself.
4. **Soothe and support.** Talk to yourself kindly. Fierce self-compassion helps you get back up.
5. **Learn.** Analyze specifics like a coach. What worked? What didn't? Turn failure into wisdom.
6. **Take action.** Apologize effectively if needed. Make amends through changed behaviour. Practice self-forgiveness.
7. **Reconnect with your values.** Decide next steps based on what matters, not just the pain.

### Making Self-Criticism Useful

Remove these from your self-talk: global attacks on your character, overgeneralizing, recalling every past failure, contemptuous language or name-calling, and avoidance or blame.

Include these instead: pick your perspective (see the bigger picture), evaluate actions only (not your worth), choose words carefully, be honest but not harsh, and shift focus from self-evaluation to contributing to others.

#### Section Summary

- Mistakes and regrets are human. How you react matters most. Your worth is separate.
- Practice bouncing back with self-kindness, learning, and refocusing on values.
- Make self-criticism constructive: focus on specific actions, choose words carefully, be honest but kind, and face the world.

## Feeling Good Enough (Comparison, Belonging & Saying No)

Many fear that self-acceptance leads to laziness or settling. But research shows self-acceptance and self-kindness actually lead to more resilience, trying again after failure, and more confidence. You strive from a place of feeling okay, not from fear of not being good enough.

Self-acceptance means knowing and accepting all parts of yourself - strengths and struggles - listening to your needs, acting in your own best interest, and being kind to yourself after mistakes.

### The Comparison Trap

Comparing ourselves socially is normal, but it becomes destructive easily. Social media, with its curated and unrealistic images, breeds dissatisfaction and envy. Comparing with loved ones is especially damaging - it creates resentment and destroys trust.

When envy arises, see it as a signal about your own life direction and satisfaction. Be curious about the feeling rather than acting on the impulse to diminish others.

Making comparison constructive:
- Focus on a specific skill you admire and want to learn, not the whole person.
- Use a growth mindset - believe you can improve with effort.
- Keep your self-worth separate from how you measure up.

### Belonging vs. Fitting In

Fitting in means changing yourself to be accepted. Belonging means being accepted for who you truly are. Seek belonging in places that align with your values, not groups that require you to be someone else.

Take responsibility for initiating connection rather than waiting passively. Shift focus from "Am I welcome?" to "How can I make others feel welcome?".

### Saying No & Setting Boundaries

Saying yes to avoid guilt often leads to resentment. Learn to tolerate guilt when saying no aligns with your needs or values. Guilt doesn't automatically mean you're wrong.

Anchor your decisions in your own values. Prioritize self-respect over others' approval. Practice assertiveness: state your understanding neutrally, express your feelings about specific behaviours, be direct, and seek fair compromises.

#### Section Summary

- True self-worth comes from self-acceptance and living by your values, not constant comparison or pleasing others.
- Use comparison constructively for inspiration, keeping self-worth separate.
- Seek belonging where you're accepted for you; be proactive in connecting.
- Learn to say no by tolerating guilt and prioritizing self-respect over approval.
